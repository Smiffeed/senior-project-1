%--------------------------ABSTRACT-------------------------------------
{\paragraph{Abstract.} This study develops an initial Thai profanity detection model and, more importantly, identifies effective evaluation settings for practical censorship. We fine-tune a Thai wav2vec~2.0 classifier and perform a systematic parameter study over window sizes (0.3--2.0\,s) and two stride schemes\textemdash{}fixed 0.05\,s and percentage-of-window\textemdash{}assessed under four protocols: (i) window-level classification, (ii) merged-word evaluation, (iii) merged-word with Intersection-over-Union (IoU) thresholds, and (iv) variants incorporating voice activity detection (VAD) for boundary refinement. Across a large grid of configurations, three findings emerge: shorter windows (\textasciitilde{}0.3\,s) improve word-level localization; percentage-based strides maintain consistent overlap across window sizes; and merging with an IoU threshold stabilizes decisions relative to raw window predictions. VAD further trims silence and bridges brief gaps, yielding cleaner, censorable spans. The outcome is an initial, deployable model together with concrete, evidence-based parameter recommendations and a reusable evaluation framework. These results reduce ad hoc tuning, guide future work on Thai profanity detection under data scarcity, and accelerate real-world deployment of automated beep-censoring systems.}

\section{Introduction}
Content moderation remains a critical challenge for digital media platforms that host large volumes of explicit content, especially in languages with limited computational resources such as Thai. While automated profanity detection systems are well-researched for languages like English in both text \cite{Davidson2017} and audio \cite{Wazir2022}, Thai presents unique computational challenges due to its tonal nature and complex phonetic structure \cite{TheeraUmpon2011}, as well as the scarcity of large-scale profanity-labeled datasets. Moreover, traditional manual censorship is still widely used, but it is time-consuming, inconsistent, and impractical for large-scale content moderation.

Recent advances in speech recognition and deep learning have enabled more sophisticated content moderation tools. However, most existing models are trained on non-Thai datasets, resulting in higher error rates when applied to Thai profanity detection. Our work addressed these challenges by fine-tuning a pre-trained wav2vec~2.0 model \cite{Baevski2020} specifically for Thai profanity detection through supervised learning with sequence classification. The base model used was \texttt{airesearch/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53-th}, which had been pre-trained on Thai speech data using self-supervised learning techniques.

While this initial system demonstrated promising results, its performance was constrained by limited training data, time, and computational resources. A fundamental issue in low-resource language processing is that deep learning models typically require extensive labeled datasets to achieve optimal performance—often impractical to obtain for specialized tasks like profanity detection \cite{Kenton2019}.

The development of robust Thai profanity detection models faces this central constraint: the lack of extensive labeled datasets. Although ASR-based pipelines show promise, they struggle with Thai's phonetic diversity, slang variations, and tonal ambiguities, leading to inconsistent profanity detection accuracy. More critically, the evaluation methodologies used to assess these systems often lack systematic analysis of temporal parameters—specifically window size and stride—that significantly impact detection performance in practical deployment scenarios.

Traditional evaluation approaches in speech processing often use fixed parameters without exploring the relationship between temporal segmentation settings and detection accuracy. In profanity detection, the choice of analysis window size and stride directly affects both detection sensitivity and temporal precision, yet existing evaluation methods rarely provide guidance on appropriate stride for parameter optimization \cite{Wazir2022}, especially for Thai where phoneme and tone differences are critical.

Recognizing the data limitation as an inherent challenge rather than a solvable problem within the scope of our research, this project shifts focus from purely model development to comprehensive evaluation methodology and parameter optimization. The central question becomes: given limited training data, how can we systematically identify the most effective evaluation approaches and optimal system parameters (window size and stride configurations) to guide future research and practical implementation?

This project centers on the comprehensive evaluation and optimization of Thai profanity detection systems in audio content. Building upon the foundational fine-tuned wav2vec~2.0 model developed in Senior Project 1, we design and implement multiple evaluation methodologies to assess detection performance and produce meaningful evaluation results. Concretely, we systematically explore the impact of critical temporal parameters—window size (ranging from 0.3\,s to 2.0\,s) and stride configurations (as a percentage of window length and fixed increments of 0.05\,s)—across different parameter combinations.

This research addresses a practical need by identifying optimal window and stride parameters for different evaluation methods. Our contributions are twofold: (1) Optimal parameter identification across evaluation methods, providing concrete guidance for future implementations; and (2) An extensible evaluation system framework that future researchers can directly apply to assess Thai profanity detection models consistently and reliably. The practical impact is that developers can immediately adopt effective configurations (e.g., short windows such as 0.3\,s for word-level tasks) instead of spending significant time on ad hoc parameter exploration, accelerating the development of Thai language content moderation systems.

\section{Related Work}
Profanity detection in speech has been a significant research area in speech processing and NLP. Existing methods generally fall into three main categories: (1) ASR-based text processing \cite{Jacobs2023,An2024}, (2) direct speech-based profanity detection using deep learning \cite{An2024,Wazir2022}, and (3) text-based hate speech \cite{Hemtanon2024} and keyword-based approaches \cite{Aljumily2023}. This section overviews these approaches and their relevance to profanity detection in Thai speech, with emphasis on evaluation methodologies and parameter optimization that inform our research.

Traditional pipelines convert speech to text using Automatic Speech Recognition (ASR) and then apply text-based profanity detection algorithms \cite{Davidson2017}. While this leverages mature text processing techniques and extensive text-based profanity datasets, it is unable to precisely locate timestamps in audio, which limits downstream censoring applications that require accurate time spans.

Recent advances in deep learning enable direct processing of audio for speech classification, bypassing ASR. Convolutional and recurrent architectures have been applied on spectrograms for classification tasks including profanity \cite{Wazir2022}. More recently, self-supervised models such as wav2vec~2.0 \cite{Baevski2020} operate directly on raw waveforms and offer precise temporal localization of acoustic features. This direct analysis allows fine-grained windowing strategies that can capture the exact boundaries of profane words—crucial for accurate detection and subsequent censoring. Configurable window sizes make such models particularly suitable for tasks where precise temporal localization is essential.

Keyword spotting focuses on detecting predefined words or phrases in continuous audio streams with low latency and compact models \cite{9665775}. The technique is modern CNN-based small-footprint systems that operate on log-mel features \cite{Sainath2015KWS}. KWS-style modeling and post-processing (e.g., merging adjacent positive frames/windows) provide complementary insights for profanity detection, especially for obtaining robust word-level spans under resource constraints.

Prior work explores limited segment lengths and overlaps, e.g., 0.3--0.5\,s windows with overlapping strides to reduce word-splitting \cite{Wazir2022}. Frame-level methods evaluate with localization metrics such as IoU \cite{An2024}, but require frame-wise annotations, which are resource-intensive—especially in low-resource languages like Thai. Our study systematizes window/stride choices across evaluation methods to provide practical guidance for downstream censoring.

Evaluation methodologies in speech processing commonly include window-based and frame-based paradigms. In window-based evaluation, continuous audio is segmented into fixed-size windows and each segment is classified independently. For instance, \cite{Wazir2022} systematically examined window lengths of 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5\,s for film censorship and found improved F1 and accuracy with longer segments, concluding 0.5\,s as optimal in their setting. They also addressed the word-splitting issue of fixed windows by introducing overlapping windows (stride < 100\%), which reduced false negatives. However, they did not broadly analyze stride values, leaving open the optimal relationship between window size and stride—an explicit focus of our work. Beyond profanity detection, event-detection literature also motivates windowing and overlapping strategies for robustness \cite{Mesaros2016,Piczak2015}.

Frame-level evaluation offers finer temporal resolution by predicting at short frame intervals (e.g., 10\,ms), producing detailed time-stamped rationales for events (e.g., hate speech), and scoring localization quality via metrics like IoU \cite{An2024}. This granularity enables precise temporal alignment but depends on framewise annotations, which are costly to obtain—particularly for low-resource languages. In contrast, window-based evaluation works well with more common word-level or clip-level labels, aligning with our objective to optimize practical profanity detection. Accordingly, we adopt window-based evaluation to conduct a systematic, large-scale study of window and stride configurations without requiring exhaustive frame-level labels, targeting deployable performance and speed.

\subsection{Data Processing}
We match the deployed and evaluation pipelines to the training-time preprocessing used for fine-tuning \texttt{airesearch/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53-th}. For each windowed slice, we load the exact segment via frame offsets to avoid drift, apply a first-order pre-emphasis filter ($\mathrm{coef}=0.97$), and perform a gentle energy-based gate by attenuating samples below $2.5\times$ the 20th-percentile amplitude. To reduce boundary artifacts without suppressing content, we use a light Hamming blend, followed by mild dynamic range compression via soft clipping around $2\times$ RMS to stabilize dynamics across windows. Audio is then resampled to 16 kHz mono if needed, normalized by z-scoring (mean/std) and scaled by 0.5 to match training amplitude statistics, and finally padded or truncated to the exact window length in samples (e.g., 0.5\,s $\Rightarrow$ 8000 samples at 16 kHz). For training, we segment windows directly from labeled timestamps. For evaluation and real usage, we slide windows with overlap to avoid splitting words \cite{Wazir2022}, merge adjacent positives into word-level spans, and optionally refine boundaries with a simple VAD (\texttt{librosa.effects.split}, \texttt{top\_db}=20, merging gaps $\leq$50 ms and adding $\pm$0.1 s padding within the original span).

\subsection{Model}
We adopt a Thai wav2vec~2.0 backbone pre-trained via self-supervised learning \cite{Baevski2020} and fine-tune it for multi-class sequence classification (four profanity types plus none). Input raw waveforms are encoded by a CNN feature encoder and transformer stack; sequence representations are mean-pooled and passed to a classifier.

\subsection{Training}
Our training methodology leverages transfer learning to address the low-resource nature of Thai profanity detection. We fine-tune the Thai wav2vec~2.0 backbone (\texttt{airesearch/wav2vec2-large-xlsr-53-th}), which provides strong speech representations learned from general Thai audio, and adapt it to multi-class profanity classification. Training is configured to balance learning efficiency and generalization: a batch size of 16 offers stable gradients within available compute; a learning rate of $5\times10^{-5}$ with linear decay enables fine-grained parameter updates without overshooting; and AdamW with weight decay regularizes the parameter space to reduce overfitting. To further handle class imbalance, we apply class weighting as defined in Eqs.~(\ref{eq:weighted-loss})--(\ref{eq:class-weight}), which amplifies the contribution of underrepresented classes. We train for up to 100 epochs with early stopping based on validation performance to prevent premature convergence or overfitting.

To better capture transitions at window boundaries, we pad each training window by 0.2\,s at the edges. We also employ light data augmentation to improve robustness: time stretching (\(\pm10\%\)), pitch shifting (\(\pm2\) semitones), background noise mixing at varying SNRs, and time masking. The dataset is split 80/20 with stratification to maintain label distribution across train and validation sets.

\paragraph{Data augmentation.} We adopt a targeted but comprehensive augmentation suite to expose the model to realistic acoustic variability likely to occur in production: (i) time stretching within a \(\pm10\%\) range to accommodate speaking rate differences; (ii) pitch shifting by \(\pm2\) semitones to simulate speaker characteristics and vocal ranges; (iii) addition of background noise across multiple signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs) to improve robustness in noisy environments; and (iv) random time masking to discourage over-reliance on specific temporal cues and promote invariance to local dropouts.

\paragraph{Validation and model selection.} We use an 80/20 train/validation split with stratified sampling to preserve class distribution across profanity categories. During training, we monitor validation metrics epoch-by-epoch and adjust hyperparameters if instability is observed. Early stopping uses validation performance as the stopping criterion, selecting the checkpoint that maximizes validation performance while preventing overfitting, thereby yielding a model with better generalization to unseen audio.

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{weighted}} = - \sum_{c=1}^{C} w_c\, y_c\, \log(p_c)
\label{eq:weighted-loss}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
w_c = \frac{N}{C\, N_c}

\end{equation}

\subsection{Evaluation Methodology}
Our evaluation framework is designed for real-world censoring use cases. It assesses performance across a wide range of temporal parameters and four complementary methodologies, while comparing two stride strategies for sliding-window inference.

\paragraph{Stride strategies.} We investigate two ways to advance the analysis window through time:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Fixed-time stride:} a constant step of 0.05\,s between consecutive windows.
  \item \textbf{Percentage-based stride:} a relative step (e.g., 10\%, 25\%) of the window length so that overlap scales with window size.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Window Evaluation}
This is the most direct protocol and operates at the window level. For each audio file, we pre-process the ground truth by labeling an analysis window as \emph{profanity} if its temporal overlap with a profane word span is at least 50\%. The model then classifies each window, and predictions are compared to the corresponding ground-truth window labels. Performance is reported with standard classification metrics (e.g., Accuracy, F1-score) at the window level.

\subsubsection{Merged-Word Evaluation}
This method reconstructs a single profanity event from multiple, consecutive window predictions under the assumption that a spoken profane word may span several overlapping windows. Consecutive windows predicted as the same positive class are merged into a continuous time segment. For example, with a 0.5\,s window and 0.25\,s stride, four overlapping positive windows covering [2.00, 3.25]\,s would merge into a single predicted profanity event [2.00, 3.25]\,s. The merged prediction is then compared against the ground-truth word span to evaluate whether the complete duration was captured.

\subsubsection{Merged-Word IoU Evaluation}
This variant enhances the merging approach by using Intersection-over-Union (IoU) as the primary criterion for a successful detection. After merging consecutive positive windows into a predicted word instance, we compute IoU with the ground-truth word span; the prediction is counted as a true positive (TP) if IoU exceeds a threshold (e.g., 0.5). Prediction gaps are treated as separators: non-contiguous positives yield distinct predicted word instances, each evaluated independently with its own IoU and TP/FP decision. For instance, consistent predictions that fully tile a ground-truth span yield IoU = 1.0, whereas [positive, none, positive] produces two separate instances with separate IoUs.

\subsubsection{Merged-Word IoU Area Combination Evaluation}
This region-centric protocol evaluates all predictions associated with a single ground-truth instance collectively. For each ground-truth profanity span, we combine the total duration of all predicted profanity segments that fall within that span, even if they are discontinuous, and compute a single IoU for the region. Example: predictions [2.00, 3.00] and [4.00, 5.00] within a ground-truth span [2.00, 5.00] yield an intersection of 2.00\,s and a union of 3.00\,s, so $\mathrm{IoU} = 2.00/3.00 \approx 0.67$. With an IoU threshold of 0.5, this is counted as a TP for the entire ground-truth region. This method rewards models that identify substantial portions of a profane word even if predictions are fragmented.

\section{Results}
We conducted a broad search over window sizes (0.3--2.0\,s) and both stride strategies, evaluating four methodologies to understand temporal trade-offs and identify operating points suitable for censoring.

\subsection{Model Performance}
\subsubsection{Evaluation Methodology Selection}
Before presenting quantitative results, we first selected the most appropriate evaluation methodology. We implemented and compared four approaches: (1) Window Evaluation (direct window classification), (2) Merged-Word Evaluation (merge overlapping positives into word spans), (3) Merged-Word IoU Evaluation (merge then match by IoU threshold), and (4) Merged-Word IoU Area Combination Evaluation (region-centric IoU over all predictions within a ground-truth span). An important observation is that plain Merged-Word Evaluation can artificially inflate F1-scores by focusing evaluation only on regions near annotated profane words, which biases precision upward. To avoid this bias and ensure objective temporal matching, we adopt the IoU-based protocols (3) and (4) as primary, due to their (i) objective overlap quantification, (ii) absence of evaluation bias, (iii) reproducibility, (iv) alignment with temporal detection literature, and (v) clear applicability to real-world censoring when paired with VAD.

\subsubsection{IoU Threshold Selection}
We performed a threshold sensitivity analysis to select the IoU threshold for the IoU-based methods. As the IoU threshold increases, F1 decreases as expected, since stricter temporal alignment reduces recall. Across binary and multiclass settings, the highest F1 is achieved at a lenient threshold of 0.1, indicating that the model often captures portions of profane spans without perfectly aligning full duration. Therefore, we use $\mathrm{IoU}=0.1$ for all IoU-based results reported below.

\subsubsection{Stride Selection}
We examined stride by heatmaps over window and stride for both fixed-time and percentage-based advances. For binary classification under the IoU Area Combination protocol, large windows (e.g., 2.0\,s) with large strides can yield higher binary F1, reflecting robust presence detection but less precise boundaries. Conversely, multiclass results favor shorter windows with substantial overlap (e.g., 0.3\,s with 80\% stride), consistent with the need for finer temporal resolution. For the IoU Area Combination method, a 1.2\,s window with an \(\sim\)80--90\% stride yielded a good trade-off in our runs.


\subsubsection{IoU Percentage Analysis}
\begin{Figure}
	\begin{center}
		\includegraphics[width=8cm]{img/mean_iou.png}
	\end{center}

  \label{fig:mean_iou}
\end{Figure}
Using the IoU Area Combination metric, we summarized mean IoU across window/stride grids. Smaller windows (0.3--0.5\,s) generally produce higher mean IoU than larger windows, confirming that finer segmentation improves boundary localization. As an illustrative example for a single ground-truth span [2.00, 5.00]\,s, discontinuous predictions [2.00, 3.00] and [4.00, 5.00] yield an intersection of 2.00\,s and a union of 3.00\,s, giving $\mathrm{IoU}\approx0.67$, which would count as a true positive at a 0.1 threshold. Aggregated results show best mean IoU at small windows with appropriately scaled strides, with fixed 0.05\,s steps yielding competitive performance at 0.3\,s windows.

As illustrated in Fig.\ref{fig:mean_iou}, there is a clear inverse relationship between window size and mean IoU. Smaller window configurations (0.3--0.5\,s) consistently outperform larger windows across both evaluation types. The fixed-step condition (\texttt{eval\_by\_0.05}) shows a slight advantage at small windows, peaking at a combined mean IoU of 29.19\% for a 0.3\,s window with a 0.25\,s stride. Confidence intervals widen for larger windows, indicating higher variance and less consistent temporal localization as window duration increases. These observations support the hypothesis that shorter analysis windows provide more precise boundary detection for profanity segments.

Focusing on window effects, three operating points highlight the trade-off between detection and temporal localization: (i) a large 2.0\,s window with a 1.9\,s stride maximizes binary F1 (47.05\%) but yields a low IoU (9.16\%), indicating coarse boundaries; (ii) a short 0.3\,s window with an 80\% stride gives the best multiclass F1 (52.34\%) with improved IoU (24.90\%); and (iii) a 0.3\,s window with a 70\% stride attains the highest IoU (25.34\%) with strong multiclass F1 (51.17\%). Overall, 0.3\,s windows consistently tighten temporal boundaries and improve IoU, while very large windows favor presence detection at the expense of precise spans.




\subsection{Real-World Usage Recommendations}
Recommendations depend on deployment goals:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Presence detection for censoring decisions (binary): larger windows (e.g., 2.0\,s) with IoU-based merging often yield higher binary F1, and VAD post-processing can further tighten spans.
  \item Timestamp accuracy and localization (multiclass or span extraction): shorter windows (e.g., 0.3\,s) with substantial overlap perform better on IoU and multiclass F1; use merged-word IoU methods with a low IoU threshold (0.1) and optional VAD refinement.
  \item Balanced operating point: under the IoU Area Combination protocol, a 1.2\,s window with \(\sim\)80--90\% stride provided a reasonable balance between binary and multiclass F1 in our experiments.
\end{itemize}

\section{Future Work and Limitations}
\subsection{Future Work}
\paragraph{Context-aware hierarchical models.} Our current approach is purely acoustic. A promising direction is a two-stage system in which the acoustic model proposes profanity candidates and a language model performs context-aware verification, helping distinguish genuine slurs from innocuous mentions.

\paragraph{Advanced boundary refinement with neural VAD.} We currently use an energy-based VAD for boundary cleanup. A neural VAD trained to separate speech from complex background noise (music, traffic) could improve timestamp precision and reduce false trims.

\paragraph{Real-time streaming architecture.} Extending the offline pipeline to streaming requires buffer management, low-latency inference, and on-the-fly windowing/merging, enabling applications like live broadcast moderation.

\subsection{Limitations}
\paragraph{Sensitivity to noise.} Energy-based VAD can be brittle in noisy conditions, and loud non-speech sounds may be misclassified as speech; very quiet profanity may be missed.

\paragraph{Lack of linguistic context.} Without text or semantics, the system censors based solely on acoustic cues and cannot disambiguate quoted or educational usage from insults.

\paragraph{Data scarcity.} Limited training data constrains generalization, especially across colloquial variants. Enlarging and diversifying the dataset remains a central need.

\section{Conclusion}
We present a Thai profanity detection system and comprehensive parameter study using a fine-tuned wav2vec~2.0 model. Our framework spans preprocessing, sliding-window inference, merging, IoU-based evaluation, and optional VAD refinement, providing a practical pipeline for automated censoring. Through extensive experiments over windows and strides, we identify evaluation biases in plain merging, motivate IoU-based methodologies with a 0.1 threshold, and offer scenario-specific configurations: large windows for presence detection, short windows for precise localization, and a balanced 1.2\,s operating point for mixed objectives. While dataset scale and noise sensitivity limit performance, the system demonstrates the viability of automated profanity moderation in Thai audio and establishes a reproducible foundation for future work in context-aware modeling, neural VAD, and real-time streaming.
